Deep learning for dynamical systems analysis

The swinging Atwood's machine and its Poincaré map: four regular orbits trace rings, one chaotic orbit (green) fills the sea between them.

Poincaré maps can be used to analyze dynamical systems. They have an interesting property in that different system parameters (say, the mass of a pendulum) result in human-perceptible, surprisingly smoothly changing characteristics in the map, even if the underlying trajectories integrated to obtain it are highly chaotic.

This is notable since estimating the parameters of a dynamical system from trajectory data has proven challenging for chaotic systems. We show that a ResNet can exploit those smoothly changing visual characteristics to regress a system's parameters, given a training set of maps for the same system at various parameter values, and that basic data augmentation handles the practical problems: predictions on trajectories integrated for different lengths of time, or from fewer initial conditions.

ATOM-1

Twelve RNA folds, from tRNA to the hammerhead ribozyme and the TPP riboswitch, each with its PDB code.

Protein language models and MSAs give AlphaFold and ESM crucial hints about inter-residue interactions. For mammalian mRNA, RNA MSAs are a poor substitute: they're shallow with only four bases, tangled by intron and exon structure, and confounded by codon-level signal.

Chemical probing data is different. It reports, nucleotide by nucleotide, whether a base was accessible in solution, it can be collected across millions of sequences, and a model trained to predict it from sequence learns embeddings that simple probes turn into strong performance on downstream structure tasks.

Pure exploration in bandit problems

Imagine that your friend has just started a small online store selling elaborately embroidered sweaters. Setting up production for a new design is slow and expensive, so they'll make a large batch of one design at a time, and they'd like to find the most popular one by showing candidate designs to visitors and asking them to rate their interest. Show each design uniformly at random and you waste views on obvious losers. The setting where you only pay for the final choice, not for exploring, is the one that gave the world Hyperband.

Ab initio quantum chemistry

PsH is an exotic molecule: a proton and a positron bound together with two electrons, believed to have been observed in the 1990s. Its binding energy is tiny, which makes it a hard test for the variational method, and the properties of interest, like its decay into a proton, an electron and one or zero photons, need integrals that only a very old, very fast language will do.
